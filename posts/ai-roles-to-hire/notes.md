## Brainstorm Summary

This post makes a two-part argument. First: the AI roles worth hiring are not the ones with the most impressive titles. They are the ones that take the repeatable, blocking work off your plate — the research you defer, the draft you procrastinate, the inbox digest that never happens. Second: when you staff these roles correctly, they don't diminish your expertise. They extend what you can do with it. The thesis is anti-hype but optimistic — not "AI will replace you," not "AI is overrated," but "here is what becomes possible when you stop treating it as a chatbot and start treating it as a set of governed operating functions."

The structure follows a 5-department org chart: Intelligence Analyst, Growth Operator, Voice Operator, Builder Partner, and Operating Chief of Staff. Strategy Sparring sits across all of them as a mode, not a standalone hire. This framing is deliberately chosen over a flat 8-role list because it gives solopreneurs, managers, and executives a mental model they can map to their own context — everyone has an intelligence function, a growth function, a comms function. The org chart makes it concrete without being prescriptive.

The centrepiece mechanic — the thing that separates a well-run AI role from "I asked ChatGPT to help" — is writing a job description before you build anything. Goal, inputs, allowed tools, forbidden actions, escalation triggers, human owner, quality bar. This is what turns a vague prompt into a governed assistant. The post will walk through this as a practical template readers can use immediately.

The audience is solopreneurs, managers, and executives. All three have a version of every department. The post should serve each without making the others feel like it isn't for them. Solopreneurs see the solo operator stack; managers see what they can delegate to AI before growing headcount; executives see the operating leverage question. The human expertise angle is important throughout — every role section should land on what the human brings that the agent cannot.

Evidence strength from the research: Intelligence Analyst and Voice Operator are the strongest (well-documented real-world workflows). Growth Operator and Builder Partner are strong-to-medium. Operating Chief of Staff is medium — credible when connected to real tools (inbox, calendar, Slack, docs), overhyped when treated as a general-purpose executive assistant. The article should reflect this honestly rather than overselling every role equally.

## Rough Table of Contents

- **Introduction: The wrong question** — Most people ask "which AI tools should I use?" The better question is "what roles should I hire, and what should they do?" Sets up the org chart framing and the two-part thesis.

- **The hype trap: why AI executive titles are the wrong starting point** — "AI CFO," "AI CMO," "AI CEO" — why these names set you up to fail. The title is not the point; the workflow is the point. Introduces the naming principle: task-descriptive over executive-sounding.

- **The AI org chart: five departments, one operating system** — Overview of the 5-department structure. Brief map of what each department handles and who it serves across the three audience types. Strategy Sparring introduced as cross-cutting mode.

- **Department 1 — The Intelligence Analyst** — Market research, competitor tracking, customer insight synthesis. What it does, what a good job description looks like, where it fails without clear inputs. Evidence: Clay workflows, Dovetail, Relevance AI. Human expertise angle: the analyst frames the questions; the agent finds the answers.

- **Department 2 — The Growth Operator** — Sales research and qualification, support triage. Two distinct functions under one growth umbrella. Scoped outreach drafts vs. autonomous sending — the human gate. Evidence: Harmonic/Clay SDR workflow, Lindy. Human expertise angle: judgment on fit, tone, and escalation stays human.

- **Department 3 — The Voice Operator** — Content drafting, brand voice enforcement, publishing pipeline. The role that most solopreneurs already use informally — this section makes it formal and governed. Evidence: n8n/Make content workflows. Human expertise angle: the voice is still yours; the operator executes it at scale.

- **Department 4 — The Builder Partner** — Product spec writing, coding assistant, QA partner. Strongest for technical builders; accessible to non-technical managers with the spec-writing function. Evidence: Codex, Claude Code, Cursor. Human expertise angle: the builder still owns the design and the decisions.

- **Department 5 — The Operating Chief of Staff** — Meeting prep, inbox digest, project change monitoring, stakeholder follow-up drafts. Most useful when connected to real systems (calendar, email, Slack, docs). Evidence: Lindy, n8n, Zapier. Human expertise angle: the human still runs the meeting, makes the call, and owns the relationship.

- **The centrepiece: write a job description before you build anything** — The AI job description template in full: goal, inputs, allowed sources, allowed tools, forbidden actions, output format, escalation triggers, human owner, quality bar, examples. Why skipping this is the number one reason AI roles underperform. Practical walkthrough with one role as an example.

- **Redefining the art of the possible** — What changes when you have all five departments running. Not "you work less" but "what you can do with your expertise expands." Concrete examples per audience type. The human-in-the-loop principle restated: agents prepare, suggest, summarise, draft — humans decide, approve, and own the consequences.

- **Where to start: first hires by audience type** — Solopreneur: Voice Operator + Intelligence Analyst first. Manager: Operating Chief of Staff + Growth Operator. Executive: Intelligence Analyst + Builder Partner. One practical first step each.

- **Closing: the operating system metaphor** — The AI org chart is not a novelty. It is an operating system for your work. You still run it. But now it runs better.
