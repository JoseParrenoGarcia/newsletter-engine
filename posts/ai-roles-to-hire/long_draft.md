# 5 AI roles every solopreneur, manager, and executive should hire

A few months ago, I was talking to a friend who runs a small consultancy. Smart operator. Follows every AI newsletter. Had tried ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini. Spent real time with all of them.

I asked what had actually changed about how he works. He thought for a moment.

"Honestly? Not much. I use it for quick drafts sometimes. But it's not like I have a new colleague."

He was asking the wrong question. Most people are. The conversation about AI at work has been dominated by tools — which model, which app, which subscription. That is the wrong frame. Tools do not change how work gets done. Roles do.

The question worth asking is not "which AI tool should I use?" It is: "What roles should AI play in my work, and who owns the outputs?"

That reframe changes everything. A tool you open when you remember is a convenience. A role with clear inputs, a defined job, and a human owner is an operating function. One of them changes your week. The other is a tab you sometimes visit.

This post is about the roles worth hiring. Not the ones with the most impressive titles — those are traps, and I will explain why in a moment. The roles worth hiring are the ones that handle the repeatable blocking work: the research you defer, the draft you procrastinate, the brief that never gets written because you are already in the next meeting.

When you staff them correctly, they do not replace your expertise. They extend what you can do with it. That is the argument I want to make here, and the evidence for it is more concrete than most "AI at work" writing admits.

## Why are AI executive titles the wrong starting point?

AI executive titles — "AI CFO," "AI CMO," "AI CEO" — are a category error. They imply a role that sets strategy, owns stakeholder relationships, and is accountable for outcomes across a function. No AI agent does any of that today. When you name a role after an executive, you scope it like one: vague inputs, diffuse responsibility, and an agent that does everything weakly because it was never designed to do anything well.

Open LinkedIn on any given week and you will find someone announcing their "AI Chief Marketing Officer," their "AI CFO," or, inevitably, their "AI CEO."

These names are not harmless branding. They are a category error that leads directly to building the wrong thing.

An executive title implies a role that sets strategy, makes judgment calls, owns stakeholder relationships, and is accountable for outcomes across an entire function. No AI agent does any of those things today. What AI agents do well is a much narrower set of tasks: research, synthesis, drafting, structuring, classifying, summarising, flagging. Useful work. Real leverage. But a different animal entirely from the role of a Chief Financial Officer.

When you name something "AI CFO," you are implicitly designing it to behave like an executive. That means scope creep, vague inputs, diffuse responsibility, and eventually an agent that does everything weakly because it was never scoped to do anything well.

The naming principle that works is the opposite: task-descriptive over executive-sounding. Not "AI VP of Sales" — "Sales Research Assistant." Not "AI CMO" — "Content Operator." Not "AI Chief of Staff" — "Meeting Prep and Inbox Summary Agent."

The name should describe what the role produces, not what the role would be called if it wore a lanyard at a leadership offsite.

This is not pedantry. The name shapes the job description, the job description shapes the inputs, and the inputs determine whether the agent actually delivers value. A vague title produces a vague brief. A vague brief produces a vague agent. A vague agent disappoints — and you conclude that AI is not useful, when the real conclusion is that you never scoped the role.

## What is the AI org chart as an operating system?

Every business, every team, every solopreneur running a one-person operation has five core operating functions. They may not be staffed. They may not be named. But the work exists.

Someone has to track what is happening in the market. Someone has to find and qualify the next customer. Someone has to produce the communications that represent the business. Someone has to build and maintain the product or the service. And someone has to keep the operation running — the meetings, the follow-ups, the project tracking, the things that fall through the gaps.

In most small teams, one person does all five. In most large organisations, those functions are under-resourced in specific ways — the analysis is always behind, the content pipeline is always clogged, the meeting prep is always shallow.

I have seen this directly running data science teams. The analytical function exists — but the research work that should feed strategy decisions gets squeezed between model delivery, stakeholder meetings, and incident response. The analysis that should happen weekly happens monthly, if at all.

AI roles do not replace the people doing this work. They fill the gaps. They handle the routine, structured, repeatable parts of each function so that the human doing the job can spend more time on the parts that require judgment, taste, relationships, and experience.

The five departments map directly onto this:

- **The Intelligence Analyst** — intelligence and research function
- **The Growth Operator** — sales, growth, and customer success function
- **The Voice Operator** — communications and content function
- **The Builder Partner** — product and engineering function
- **The Operating Chief of Staff** — operations and coordination function

There is also what I call **Strategy Sparring Mode** — not a standalone hire, but a cross-cutting capability. A reasoning partner for working through decisions, stress-testing assumptions, or thinking through a problem before committing to a direction. It does not replace strategic thinking. It sharpens it.

This framing is useful because it maps to work that already exists. You are not adding new jobs. You are building better support for the jobs already being done — often poorly, often late, often by someone who should be spending that time differently.

## How do you write an AI role's job description before you build anything?

Write the job description before you build the role. Define the goal in one sentence, list the inputs the agent receives every run, specify which tools it can use and which actions require human approval, describe the exact output format, and name a single human owner who reviews and approves every output. This document becomes the system prompt. Without it, you have not hired a role — you have opened a chat window and hoped for the best.

This is the step most people skip. It is also the step that determines whether any of this works.

The most common AI workflow in practice looks like this: someone opens a chat interface, types a vague request, gets a generic output, concludes it is "not quite right," tries to fix it by adding more words to the prompt, gets a slightly less generic output, and eventually gives up or keeps a half-useful draft.

This is not an AI limitation. It is a job description problem. You would not hire a contractor and say "help out with the business." You would tell them exactly what you need, what good looks like, what is out of scope, and who to escalate to when something goes wrong. An AI role deserves the same clarity.

Before building any of the five roles below, write a job description. It does not need to be long. It needs to cover:

| Field | What to include |
|---|---|
| **Goal** | One sentence: what this role exists to produce |
| **Inputs** | What data, documents, or context the role receives every time it runs |
| **Allowed sources** | Which URLs, databases, or files it can draw from |
| **Allowed tools** | What actions it can take (search, read, write, send — be explicit) |
| **Forbidden actions** | What it must never do without human approval |
| **Output format** | Exactly what it produces — a doc, a table, a draft, a summary |
| **Escalation triggers** | When it must stop and surface to a human rather than continue |
| **Human owner** | One named person who reviews, approves, and owns the output |
| **Quality bar** | What does a good output look like? Include one or two examples. |

Here is what this looks like in practice, using the Intelligence Analyst as an example:

- **Goal:** Produce a weekly market brief covering competitor moves, customer language, and emerging themes relevant to [product/service]
- **Inputs:** Competitor website URLs, recent customer interview transcripts, support ticket summaries from the past 7 days, list of 3–5 questions from last week's brief
- **Allowed sources:** Listed URLs only, no social media speculation
- **Allowed tools:** Web search, summarisation, document writing — no sending or publishing
- **Forbidden actions:** Do not state conclusions about market strategy. Do not assert trends without citing a source. Do not send output to anyone.
- **Output format:** A structured brief with four sections: What changed, What customers said, What assumptions to revisit, What sources support this
- **Escalation triggers:** If a major competitor announcement appears that was not in the prior brief, flag it prominently before summarising
- **Human owner:** Jose — reviews every Monday, decides what to act on
- **Quality bar:** A good brief cites its sources, distinguishes strong signals from weak ones, and includes at least one thing that surprised me. A bad brief asserts things without evidence or restates what I already knew.

"A vague agent is a vague employee, except cheaper to create and faster to disappoint."

The job description does not go into a document and get forgotten. It becomes the system prompt or the workflow configuration for that role. Every time it runs, it runs from that brief.

Write this before you build anything. It takes thirty minutes. It will save you months of frustration.

## What does the Intelligence Analyst do?

The Intelligence Analyst handles market research, competitor tracking, and customer insight synthesis on a regular cadence. It takes structured inputs — competitor URLs, customer transcripts, support tickets — and produces a recurring brief. You review the brief and make decisions. The agent surfaces evidence; you interpret what it means. It is the right first AI role to hire because the output is concrete, the risk is low, and the value is immediate.

The Intelligence Analyst is your research function. Market research, competitor tracking, customer insight synthesis — the work that should inform every strategic decision but almost always lags behind it.

In most small teams, this work happens in one of two ways. Either someone does an intensive research sprint before a big decision, or it never happens at all. Neither is good. The first is expensive. The second means decisions get made on instinct and memory.

The Intelligence Analyst turns this into a rhythm. A weekly memo. A structured brief. Something you can actually read before Monday's planning session rather than commissioning at the last minute.

**What it does well:**
- Tracks competitor websites, pricing pages, and announcement feeds
- Summarises customer interview recordings or transcripts
- Clusters support tickets by theme and extracts recurring language
- Maps market categories from multiple sources
- Answers specific questions against a defined set of inputs

**What it needs to work:**
- A product or service description it can reference
- A list of competitors with their URLs
- Customer interview transcripts or support ticket exports
- Clear questions you want answered, not open-ended "research everything"

**Where human judgment stays essential:**
The analyst finds the answers. You frame the questions and decide what the answers mean. Market strategy — which segment to target, which competitor to take seriously, which trend to act on — remains a human call. The agent surfaces evidence; you make the argument.

For data and ML teams specifically, this role translates directly: tracking competitor model releases and benchmark claims, synthesising customer feedback about model outputs, and summarising internal experiment results into briefs that feed planning discussions. The research function is the same — the inputs are just calibration data and model performance logs instead of pricing pages and sales calls.

[n8n's AI-powered market research workflow](https://n8n.io/workflows/12236-run-ai-powered-market-research-with-groq-openai-documentero-and-gmail/) and [competitor analysis automation](https://n8n.io/workflows/6580-generate-ai-powered-competitor-analysis-reports-with-gpt-4-apify-and-google-docs/) are practical starting points. For customer research synthesis, [Dovetail's AI layer](https://dovetail.com/product/ai/) and [Relevance AI's interview synthesis templates](https://relevanceai.com/templates/customer-interview-synthesis) show what structured analysis looks like at scale.

The Intelligence Analyst is the right first hire for most people. It is low-risk, immediately useful, and builds the research foundation every other role draws from.

## What does the Growth Operator do?

The Growth Operator covers two jobs that share a common purpose: bringing in customers and keeping them.

The first is sales research and qualification. The second is support triage.

They are distinct workflows, but they belong in the same department because the skills and inputs overlap, and because the failure mode is identical: either you are moving too slowly, or you are responding without enough information.

### Sales research and qualification

The most well-documented AI workflow in practice is also one of the oldest-feeling: SDR work. Lead enrichment, account research, fit scoring, outreach drafting. [Clay](https://www.clay.com/) has built an entire platform on this. So has [Relevance AI's sales qualification template](https://relevanceai.com/templates/9101-ai-powered-lead-research-and-qualification-using-relevance-ai).

The pattern that works is not autonomous outreach. It is research-first, draft-second, human-approved-before-send.

For each inbound lead: research the company, score against your ICP, produce a short account brief, draft a personalised first message, mark confidence and flag missing information, then wait for human approval. Nothing leaves the system until a human has read it.

This is slower than full automation. It is also the difference between growth that compounds and growth that gets you marked as spam.

**What it needs:** Your ICP definition, a lead list or inbound source, outreach examples in your voice, and clear criteria for what qualifies.

### Support triage

The same research-and-draft pattern applies to support. Classify incoming tickets by urgency and type. Draft responses for routine queries. Escalate anything involving billing, legal, angry customers, refunds, or reputational risk.

The boundary is clear: the agent handles the 60-70% of tickets that are procedural. The human handles the 30-40% that require judgment, empathy, or authority.

**The hard gate:** No CRM stage updates, no external messages, no support replies for sensitive cases without human review. The [n8n AI sales assistant workflow](https://n8n.io/workflows/9026-ai-sales-assistant-with-gpt-and-claude-qualify-leads-book-meetings/) is a useful reference for how to structure the human checkpoint.

**Where human judgment stays essential:**
Fit, tone, and escalation judgment stay human. Whether this lead is worth pursuing. Whether this customer is upset or about to churn. Whether this situation requires an apology, a refund, or a conversation — those calls belong to the person who understands the relationship, not the agent that researched the account.

The tension that emerges as volume scales is worth naming: the research-first, draft-second gate works well for twenty leads a week. At two hundred, the bottleneck shifts to the human reviewer, not the agent. That is not a reason to remove the gate — it is a signal to tighten the ICP and reduce the volume that reaches the queue in the first place. The gate is not the problem. Unclear qualification criteria are.

## What does the Voice Operator do?

The Voice Operator is a governed content function. It takes rough notes, voice memos, or briefs and turns them into first drafts — consistently, against a written voice guide. The key inputs are your best previous writing as calibration and a voice guide that specifies your register, banned phrases, and what good looks like. Without those inputs, the output sounds like every other AI-assisted content on the internet. With them, it sounds like you.

Most solopreneurs already use AI for writing. A quick draft here. An edit there. A LinkedIn post when the energy runs out.

The difference between that habit and a Voice Operator is governance. The Voice Operator is a formal content function — inputs, voice guide, style constraints, output format, and a clear human review step before anything publishes.

**What it does:**
- Turns rough notes or voice memos into first drafts
- Repurposes long-form content for different channels
- Enforces style and tone against a written voice guide
- Produces newsletter outlines, internal updates, investor memos
- Offers alternative hooks, headlines, or framings before you commit

**What it needs to work:**
- Your best previous writing as calibration material
- A voice guide (even a short one: register, banned phrases, examples of good and bad)
- The intended audience and channel for each output
- A clear output format so it does not guess

The inputs are the difference between an agent that sounds like you and an agent that sounds like every other AI-assisted content on the internet. Without a voice guide, the agent defaults to an average of everything it has seen. That average is recognisable and forgettable.

[OpenAI's Custom GPTs](https://academy.openai.com/public/clubs/work-users-ynjqu/resources/custom-gpts) and [Make's AI content automation](https://www.make.com/en/ai-agents) both support this kind of persistent, voice-governed workflow. The investment is writing the voice guide once. After that, the operator runs from it.

**Where human judgment stays essential:**
The voice is yours. The claims are yours. The final editorial call is yours. The operator can produce a draft that sounds like you. It cannot decide what you actually believe, which stories are worth telling, or whether this piece is ready to publish. Those decisions stay with you — every time.

For data science leads and ML practitioners, the Voice Operator translates to a different but equally pressing writing backlog: experiment summaries for stakeholders, model decision documentation, post-incident write-ups, and the quarterly team updates that always get written at 11pm the night before. The operator does not replace the judgment about what to communicate — it eliminates the blank page that delays it.

## What does the Builder Partner do?

Coding agents are the most mature AI role in production today. The tooling has reached a real threshold — implementation workflows that would have required a junior developer two years ago are now accessible to anyone who can write a clear spec.

But the Builder Partner is not just for technical people. There are two distinct paths. [Claude Code](https://claude.ai/download), [OpenAI's Codex cloud agent](https://openai.com/blog/introducing-the-codex-cloud-agent), [GitHub Copilot](https://github.com/features/copilot), and [Cursor](https://www.cursor.com/) all support both — whether you are writing product specs or implementing code.

**The non-technical path: product spec writing**

Turn a rough idea into a PRD. Define user stories and acceptance criteria. Break a product concept into implementation tasks. Identify the risks and non-goals before a line of code is written.

This is where managers and executives who are not developers can get real value from the Builder Partner — not by having it write code, but by having it structure thinking before anyone touches a codebase. A well-written spec brief saves hours of back-and-forth with developers.

**The technical path: implementation partner**

For technical builders, the Builder Partner can implement bounded tasks, write and run tests, review diffs, flag security or edge-case risks, and open a PR for human review. It is not a senior engineer. It is a capable implementer for well-scoped, clearly specified tasks.

The operating rule is simple: it produces work, humans review and approve it. No autonomous merge. No production deploy. No security-sensitive changes without explicit review.

**What it needs:**
A repo with context (a `CLAUDE.md` or `AGENTS.md` file that explains conventions, constraints, and what good code looks like here). Examples of good PRs. Acceptance criteria written before implementation starts. Tests that define what "correct" means.

**Where human judgment stays essential:**
Architecture. Design decisions. What ships and what does not. The builder owns the implementation; the human owns the design and the consequences of the choices.

For data and ML engineers, this is where the Builder Partner becomes a serious productivity multiplier: writing data pipeline tests, generating boilerplate for new feature stores, reviewing diffs for schema migration correctness, and drafting the PR description that explains what changed and why. The code review step stays human. The drafting and testing steps do not have to.

## What does the Operating Chief of Staff do?

The Operating Chief of Staff is the most overhyped and underbuilt role in the current AI conversation.

Overhyped: because every demo of a "fully autonomous AI chief of staff" glosses over the fact that real chiefs of staff manage stakeholder politics, institutional memory, and judgment calls that are impossible to encode in a system prompt.

Underbuilt: because the narrower, more honest version — meeting prep, inbox summary, project tracking, follow-up drafts — is genuinely useful and much easier to build than the marketing suggests.

Start with the morning brief. Every morning:
1. Review today's calendar and flag meetings that need prep
2. Pull prior notes and decisions relevant to those meetings
3. Identify follow-ups due today from the previous week's commitments
4. Draft a suggested priority order for the day
5. Flag anything that has changed since yesterday

This is not glamorous. It is also the work that most managers and executives do poorly or skip entirely because it requires focused attention at the start of the day, which is exactly when the inbox has already taken over.

The difference between this role working and not working is almost always the same thing: escalation rules and real system access. Without them, the morning brief surfaces noise — a meeting that needed no prep, a follow-up that was already handled, a priority that was superseded yesterday. The agent cannot know what changed unless it is connected to the systems where things change. The manager skims one vague brief, ignores the next three, and concludes the role does not work. It did not fail. It was never given the inputs it needed.

With real calendar access, prior meeting notes, and a task tracker, the brief becomes genuinely useful. The design decision that separates the two is simple: connect the agent to the live systems, or do not build the role yet.

[Lindy's AI chief of staff templates](https://www.lindy.ai/templates/ai-chief-of-staff) and [Lenny's conversation with Webflow's CPO](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-webflows-cpo-built-an-ai-chief) about building an AI chief of staff are both worth reading. The Webflow case is instructive because it starts narrow — email triage and meeting prep — and earns its way into more complex territory over time.

**What it needs:**
Calendar access, notes from prior meetings, a task or project tracker, and explicit escalation rules for what requires human decision-making before the agent acts.

**Build it gradually.** Meeting prep first. Inbox triage second. Follow-up drafts third. Autonomous email drafting — if ever — much later, after you have calibrated its judgment against your own for months.

**Where human judgment stays essential:**
The human still runs the meeting. Still manages the relationship. Still decides what is worth escalating and what can wait. The politics, the history, the understanding of why a stakeholder behaves the way they do — none of that lives in a calendar invite. Let the agent prepare context; you still navigate the people.

## What is the human-in-the-loop principle, and why does it matter?

The human-in-the-loop principle means agents prepare, suggest, and draft — humans decide, approve, and own the consequences. It is not a safety net for when you are nervous. It is the operating model that makes AI roles sustainable. For any action that touches the world — sending a message, updating a record, publishing content, executing a transaction — a human approves it first. The agent runs the research; the human runs the operation.

Human-in-the-loop is often described as a safety check. A way of catching mistakes before they cause damage. That framing makes it sound optional — something you do when you are nervous, and skip when you are confident.

That is the wrong model. Human-in-the-loop is not a safety net. It is the operating principle that makes the entire org chart function.

The distinction is between what agents do well and what humans must own. Agents prepare, suggest, summarise, and draft. Humans decide, approve, and own the consequences.

This is not a limitation of the technology. It is a feature of any good operating model. Even in fully human organisations, there are actions that require sign-off — budgets, public communications, hires, contracts. The sign-off is not a bottleneck. It is accountability.

For AI roles, [OpenAI's practical guide to building AI agents](https://openai.com/business/guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-ai-agents/) recommends human oversight for any high-risk action. The [Anthropic guide to building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents) frames the same principle as "least privilege" — give an agent only the permissions it needs for the current task.

The specific actions that always require human approval, regardless of how confident the agent appears:

- Sending money or issuing refunds
- Sending customer emails or external messages
- Posting publicly to any channel
- Updating CRM fields or customer records
- Changing calendar invites for important meetings
- Deleting records of any kind
- Updating production configurations
- Creating pull requests that trigger deployment
- Approving contracts
- Making hiring recommendations
- Any action a legal or compliance team would want to know about

[n8n's human-in-the-loop tooling](https://docs.n8n.io/advanced-ai/human-in-the-loop-tools/) and [WEF's framework for AI agent governance](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2025/12/ai-agents-onboarding-governance/) both provide practical structure for implementing these gates.

The mental model that works: a three-tier test for every agent action.

- **Read-only** (research, summarise, analyse) — agent runs autonomously
- **Draft** (write content, prepare briefs, produce specs) — agent runs, human reviews before use
- **Send, publish, or execute** (take an action in the world) — human approves, every time

Apply this consistently across all five departments and the system is robust. Relax it in one area because it feels slow and you will eventually learn why the rule existed.

## Where should you start, by audience type?

Where you start depends on what you do. Solopreneurs: begin with the Voice Operator and Intelligence Analyst — the two roles that address the highest-frequency friction in a one-person operation. Managers: begin with the Operating Chief of Staff and the Growth Operator — meeting prep and pipeline research are structured enough to delegate immediately. Executives: begin with the Intelligence Analyst and the Builder Partner — the roles that most directly improve the quality of strategic decisions.

The five departments are the destination. Where you start depends on what you do.

### Solopreneurs

Start with the Voice Operator and the Intelligence Analyst.

The Voice Operator because content and communication are typically where solopreneurs have the most work pending and the least time. A governed Voice Operator — one with a real voice guide and clear inputs — compounds quickly. Each piece of content becomes a reference for the next.

The Intelligence Analyst because research is usually the first thing cut when time is short. A weekly market brief costs you thirty minutes to set up and gives you thirty minutes back every week, indefinitely.

**First step:** Write a voice guide. Even two pages — your register, your banned phrases, three examples of writing you are proud of. That document becomes the foundation of both roles.

### Managers

Start with the Operating Chief of Staff and the Growth Operator.

The Operating Chief of Staff because the friction in most management roles is not the decisions — it is the preparation required to make them well. Meeting prep, follow-up tracking, and project monitoring are all high-frequency, structured tasks that AI handles reliably.

The Growth Operator because managers are often the first to feel the gap between the pipeline they have and the pipeline they need. A sales research function that costs no headcount and runs on existing lead data is a fast win.

**First step:** Spend 30 minutes this week. List the five recurring meetings on your calendar. For each one, write a single sentence of context that would have helped you walk in better prepared. Those five sentences are the first brief spec for your Operating Chief of Staff — they tell you exactly what information to connect, and what format to ask for.

### Executives

Start with the Intelligence Analyst and the Builder Partner.

The Intelligence Analyst because strategic decisions made without current market intelligence are just expensive guesses. A governed research function that produces weekly briefs transforms the quality of planning sessions without adding a headcount line to the budget.

The Builder Partner because the cost of slow product development compounds. Even a non-technical executive benefits from having product specs that are more structured, risks that are surfaced earlier, and developers who spend less time on ambiguous requirements.

**First step:** List the three most important strategic decisions you will make in the next quarter. Those are your first three Intelligence Analyst briefs. Write the questions before you build anything — and be specific. "What are our competitors doing?" is not a brief. "Which of these three competitors has changed pricing or packaging in the last 60 days?" is. Spend 30 minutes writing three questions at that level of precision. That document is your starting point.

## Closing thoughts: the art of the possible

My friend — the one who had tried all the tools and felt nothing had changed — is not wrong to be sceptical. Most AI usage at work is ambient and low-leverage. A quick draft, a summarised email, a chat session that replaces a Google search. None of that changes how work gets done.

What changes things is treating AI not as a tool you open when you remember, but as a set of operating functions with clear jobs, clear inputs, clear owners, and clear limits.

The org chart framing is not a metaphor. It is a practical design pattern. You are building roles — governed, scoped, owned. You are not adding features to your workflow. You are changing what your workflow can do.

When all five departments are running, most people find they work the same hours. What changes is the ceiling on what those hours can produce. A solopreneur operates with the research depth of a small team. A manager walks into meetings with context instead of catching up in them. An executive makes strategy decisions with current intelligence rather than last quarter's memory.

None of this replaces the judgment you have built. It makes that judgment more valuable. The Intelligence Analyst brings more evidence. The evidence improves your calls. The Growth Operator qualifies leads faster. The faster qualification means more of your time goes to the customers who actually benefit from your expertise. The tools extend the range. The expertise still aims.

That is the art of the possible. Not a shorter working day. A wider range for the same working day.

You still run the operation. But now it runs better.
