Verified fact — closest public CAISI source. The closest public, primary CAISI source I
could validate for the claim in your screenshot is NIST CAISI’s Evaluation of
DeepSeek AI Models, organisation-authored by the Center for AI Standards and
Innovation, National Institute of Standards and Technology, published 30
September 2025. It is a benchmark report comparing 3 DeepSeek models with 4 U.S.
reference models across 19 benchmarks.
Verified fact — the public CAISI material retrieved here does not publish an “8
months behind” number. In the validated CAISI/NIST documents I retrieved, CAISI
reports benchmark-level performance gaps, not a public “months behind”
conversion. The report says DeepSeek lags leading U.S. models, with the largest gaps
in software engineering and cyber, but it does not expose a public formula
translating that into “6–8 months behind”.
Verified fact — an explicit month-lag estimate exists, but from Epoch AI, not the
validated CAISI report. Epoch AI’s public analysis states that Chinese AI models have
lagged the U.S. frontier by an average of 7 months since 2023, with a 4–14
month range, using the Epoch Capabilities Index rather than Elo or CAISI’s
benchmark table.
Verified fact — different evaluation systems measure different things. CAISI
measures task success on selected benchmarks plus security/censorship/adoption;
LMSYS/Arena measures human preference from pairwise votes using a Bradley–
Terry / Elo-like framework; Epoch AI uses a benchmark-stitching latent capability
index; Artificial Analysis uses a weighted composite index across 10 evaluations.
These are not interchangeable scales.
Verified fact — the practical gap is strongly task-dependent. In CAISI’s own data,
the gap is large on cyber and software-engineering tasks, but much narrower on
question-answer style knowledge benchmarks. Example: SWE-bench Verified 66.7 vs
54.8, but MMLU-Pro 90.2 vs 89.0 for best U.S. model vs DeepSeek V3.1. CAISI itself
says the question-answer benchmarks are “not by a wide margin” and are less
indicative of real-world performance than cyber and software-engineering tasks.
Verified fact — current open or open-weight models can get surprisingly close on
some coding leaderboards. On Arena’s HTML code leaderboard, the top proprietary
model in the retrieved snapshot is Claude Opus 4.7 at 1556, while the strongest
open-weight/open-licensed entries shown include GLM-5.1 at 1538, Kimi K2.6 at
1506, and DeepSeek V4 Pro Thinking at 1468. That is not parity, but neither is it a
comic-opera collapse into irrelevance.
Verified fact — for retrieval/search-enabled tasks, the retrieved public
leaderboard remains proprietary-heavy. In Arena’s Search leaderboard snapshot, the
top search-enabled models are proprietary, such as Claude Opus 4.6 Search at

•

1255 and GPT-5.5 Search at 1235; the only open model shown in the top 28 snippet
is Diffbot Small XL at 1024. That suggests the gap matters more when
search/retrieval orchestration is central.
Interpretation — the headline claim is directionally plausible, but source
attribution is shaky. “Near-frontier/open models are ~6–8 months behind” is well
aligned with Epoch AI’s explicit public analysis, but not directly substantiated by
the public CAISI documents retrieved here. The second half of the claim — “this may
not matter in practice” — is partially supported by the narrower gaps on Q&A-style
tasks and by coding leaderboards where strong open-weight models are close on
some slices, but it does not hold uniformly across cyber, retrieval/search, or agentic
engineering workloads.

What CAISI actually measures
•

•

Verified fact — CAISI capability metric is benchmark success, not Elo. The report
evaluates models across four capability domains — cyber, software engineering,
science and knowledge, mathematical reasoning — and reports accuracy /
percentage of tasks solved on the included benchmarks.
Verified fact — benchmark set. The report uses 19 benchmarks in total, including
public and CAISI/private benchmarks. The capability table includes CVE-Bench,
Cybench, CTF-Archive, SWE-bench Verified, Breakpoint, MMLU-Pro, MMMLU,
GPQA, HealthBench, Humanity’s Last Exam, SMT 2025, OTIS-AIME 2025, and
PUMaC 2024.

•

•

Verified fact — agentic evaluations were normalised. For cyber and software tasks,
CAISI used a standardised ReAct-style agent loop, a common Linux/Docker
environment, and a fixed 500,000 weighted-token budget per agentic task.
Verified fact — uncertainty modelling. CAISI estimates benchmark uncertainty
using mixed-effects logistic regression, treating model as a fixed effect and task as a
random effect; it reports error bars as estimated standard errors.

What “months behind” does and does not mean in CAISI
•

•

•

Verified fact — public CAISI wording. CAISI says DeepSeek performance “lags
behind” leading U.S. models; the best U.S. model outperforms DeepSeek V3.1 on
almost every benchmark, with the largest gaps in software engineering and cyber.
Verified fact — public CAISI does not quantify lag in months. The retrieved public
CAISI materials do not state “8 months behind” and do not publish a method for
converting benchmark gaps into elapsed calendar time.
Interpretation — likely source confusion. The phrase “about 8 months behind” is
more plausibly a secondary interpretation or a borrowing from other benchmark
frameworks such as Epoch AI’s 7-month estimate, rather than a direct quotation from
the validated NIST CAISI report.

Method assumptions and limitations
•

•

•

•

Verified fact — server path matters. CAISI evaluated proprietary U.S.
models through APIs, but downloaded DeepSeek weights from Hugging Face and
ran them on CAISI cloud servers. The report explicitly says evaluations on DeepSeek
APIs or third-party hosts may yield different results, especially for security and
censorship.
Verified fact — the report is partial and preliminary. CAISI’s disclaimer says the
findings are preliminary, limited to specific domains, model versions,
and evaluation methods, and may not generalise to other tasks. It also notes
benchmark limitations, including relevance to practical tasks and possible overlap with
training data.
Verified fact — cost comparison caveat. CAISI compares end-to-end expense, not
just token price, and explicitly notes that DeepSeek’s lower pricing came with higher
latency and a smaller maximum prompt size (64k vs “many U.S. cloud providers”
at 128k), which may make its analysis conservative in DeepSeek’s favour.
Verified fact — the report does not address all strategic questions. CAISI says it
does not investigate how models were developed, the cost of development, or
whether they were trained on distilled data.

LMSYS / Arena
•

•

•

•

•
•

Verified fact — methodology. The Chatbot Arena paper describes an open platform
for evaluating LLMs by human preference via pairwise comparisons collected from
users; Arena’s current FAQ says rankings are computed with the Bradley–Terry rating
system, similar to Elo.
Verified fact — what it measures. Arena is strongest as a measure of user-perceived
quality on open-ended prompts, rather than “objective capability” in the CAISI/Epoch
sense. Arena itself now refers to the score as Arena score rather than “Elo”, because it
is reporting Bradley–Terry coefficients on an Elo-like scale.
Verified fact — style confounds are real. Arena has published a technical note
showing that rankings can be affected by response length and markdown/style, and
that it uses style control in Bradley–Terry regression to reduce that confounding.
Verified fact — current evidence from one task slice. On Arena’s HTML code
leaderboard snapshot, proprietary leaders still occupy the top positions, but
open/open-weight models are close: Claude Opus 4.7 1556, GLM-5.1 1538, Kimi
K2.6 1506, DeepSeek V4 Pro Thinking 1468.
Agreement with CAISI. Frontier proprietary models still dominate the overall top of
the leaderboard.
Disagreement or non-overlap with CAISI. Arena is not a security/censorship
benchmark and does not provide a country-level month-lag estimate. It may show
smaller practical differences on user-facing tasks than CAISI’s cyber/softwareengineering tables.

Epoch AI
•

•

•

Verified fact — explicit month-lag result. Epoch AI’s analysis states that since 2023,
Chinese AI models have trailed the U.S. frontier by an average of 7 months, with
a minimum of 4 and a maximum of 14 months.
Verified fact — methodology. Epoch computes lag using the Epoch Capabilities
Index, which stitches benchmarks into a single capability scale. For the country-gap
analysis, it tracks the best model from each country on each day, then asks how long
since the leading U.S. model last had an ECI score as low as the best Chinese model’s
score on that day, treating scores within 1 ECI point as equivalent.
Verified fact — what ECI is. ECI uses Item Response Theory-style modelling with
benchmark difficulty and slope parameters; the current methodology page says it
draws on 37 distinct benchmarks and is fit by non-linear least squares with ridge
regularisation.

•

•
•

Verified fact — benchmark breadth. Epoch’s current methodology page lists a broad
mix of internal evaluations, independent leaderboards, and developer-reported
benchmarks, including GPQA Diamond, FrontierMath, SimpleQA Verified, Aider
polyglot, SWE-bench (Bash Only), Terminal-Bench, Cybench, MMLU, OSWorld,
and others.
Agreement with the headline claim. This is the cleanest public source in the retrieved
material for the “~6–8 months behind” statement.
Disagreement with CAISI. Epoch’s lag is a composite statistical translation across
many benchmarks, whereas CAISI publishes task-specific gaps and adds nationalsecurity evaluations such as hijacking, jailbreaking, and censorship. The two sources are
complementary, not directly comparable.

Verified fact — methodology. Artificial Analysis’ current Intelligence Index v4.0.4 is
a weighted average over 10 evaluations: GDPval-AA, τ²-Bench Telecom, TerminalBench Hard, SciCode, AA-LCR, AA-Omniscience, IFBench, Humanity’s Last Exam,
GPQA Diamond, and CritPt, grouped into four equally weighted categories.
Verified fact — current live snapshot. In the retrieved live leaderboard
snapshot, GPT-5.5 (xhigh) scores 60, while leading Chinese/open-weight or nearfrontier models include Kimi K2.6 at 54, DeepSeek V4 Pro (Max) at 52, Qwen3.6
Max Preview at 52, and gpt-oss-120B (high) at 33.
Verified fact — prior China report. Artificial Analysis’ Q2 2025 China report says the
U.S.–China frontier gap had become “as narrow now as it has ever been”, and that
the Chinese open-weight frontier surpassed the U.S. open-weight
frontier in November 2024.
Verified fact — explicit caveat. Artificial Analysis’ China report says that some results
were estimated based on company claims and comparable results, rather than all
being independently benchmarked at the moment of publication.
Agreement with the headline claim. Artificial Analysis supports the view that strong
Chinese/open-weight models are now near-frontier on several dimensions,
especially in the open-weight segment.
Disagreement with CAISI in emphasis. CAISI stresses security, censorship, and
cyber/software gaps; Artificial Analysis places more weight on general capability
composites, coding, agentic workflows, and price/performance.

Reasoning and coding
•

•

•

•

Verified fact — CAISI shows large practical gaps on software engineering. In
CAISI’s table, the best U.S. model scores 66.7 on SWE-bench Verified versus 54.8 for
DeepSeek V3.1, and 98.0 vs 78.5 on Breakpoint. CAISI’s summary says the gap is
largest for software engineering and cyber.
Verified fact — CAISI shows moderate-to-large gaps on maths. On CAISI’s maths
benchmarks, the best U.S. model scores 91.9 vs 77.6 on OTIS-AIME 2025, 85.9 vs
77.7 on PUMaC 2024, and 91.8 vs 86.2 on SMT 2025.
Verified fact — GPT-5 also improved materially over OpenAI’s prior
frontier.OpenAI’s GPT-5-for-developers launch states GPT-5 scores 74.9% on SWEbench Verified versus o3 at 69.1%, and 88% on Aider polyglot.
Verified fact — but open/open-weight models can be close on some live coding
arenas. On Arena’s HTML code leaderboard snapshot, GLM-5.1 (MIT) is only 18
Arena-score points behind Claude Opus 4.7, while Kimi K2.6 and DeepSeek V4 Pro
Thinking are also in the upper tier.
Interpretation. For coding, the gap is real but not uniform: frontier advantage is
clearer on curated engineering benchmarks and some agentic tasks than on all userfacing coding arenas.

General chat and summarisation
•

•

•

Verified fact — CAISI’s narrowest gaps are on Q&A-style knowledge
benchmarks.The best U.S. model scores 90.2 vs 89.0 on MMLU-Pro, 87.7 vs
82.2 on MMMLU, and 63.0 vs 52.5 on HealthBench relative to DeepSeek V3.1.
Verified fact — CAISI’s own interpretation is that these gaps are narrower. CAISI
says U.S. and DeepSeek models achieve similar performance on question-answerstyle science and knowledge benchmarks, with frontier models only slightly more
performant, and “not by much”.
Verified fact — human-preference frameworks are relevant here. LMSYS/Arena’s
entire methodology is based on human preference in pairwise chat comparisons,
making it a useful complement for general-chat use cases that fixed multiple-choice
benchmarks often miss.
Interpretation. This is the strongest support in the retrieved material for the claim that
“the gap may not matter in practice” — if the application is mostly drafting,
summarising, or knowledge synthesis rather than hard agentic coding or cyber tasks.

Operational complexity
•

•

•

•

Verified fact — evaluating agentic systems is operationally heavy. CAISI’s appendix
shows that agentic evaluation required a Docker/Linux environment, persistent
shells, Python execution, file tools, model-specific tool-calling optimisation, and a
standardised token budget.
Verified fact — fair evaluation itself needs engineering controls. Arena publishes
dedicated work on style control in its Bradley–Terry regression, showing that response
formatting and verbosity can bias outcomes if not corrected.
Verified fact — Artificial Analysis’ agentic benchmarks require tool and sandbox
orchestration. For GDPval-AA, Artificial Analysis runs models inside an E2B
sandboxand exposes tools such as web fetch, web search, shell execution,
and finish, then grades outputs pairwise with Elo-style scoring.
Interpretation. In real deployments, the operational burden often shifts from “which
model is smartest?” to “which stack is easiest to run, monitor, secure, and pay for?” It is
annoyingly mundane, which is exactly why it matters.

Verified fact — frontier model usage in knowledge work. OpenAI’s GPT-5.5 launch
article says more than 85% of OpenAI uses Codex every week. It also reports
internal deployments where the Comms team analysed six months of speakingrequest data, the Finance team reviewed 24,771 K-1 tax forms across 71,637 pages,
finishing two weeks faster than the prior year, and a go-to-market workflow saved 5–
10 hours per week on weekly reporting.
Verified fact — frontier buyers cite autonomy and persistence. In the same OpenAI
source, early testers such as Cursor and MagicPath attribute GPT-5.5’s value to
stronger reasoning, tool use, persistence on long-running tasks, and the ability to
carry changes through larger systems with less correction required.
Verified fact — open-weight models have strong ecosystem adoption
evidence.CAISI reports that DeepSeek model downloads on model-sharing platforms
rose by nearly 1000% since January 2025; DeepSeek API usage on OpenRouter
increased by over 5,900% from January to September 2025; and models derived from
PRC base models uploaded to Hugging Face now outnumber those derived from U.S.
developers, driven mainly by Alibaba and DeepSeek.
Verified fact — open-weight ecosystem competition is not theoretical. CAISI’s
benchmark summary says the U.S. open-weight model it evaluated, gpt-oss,
outperformed the best DeepSeek models on most agentic tasks, while the best
DeepSeek models slightly outperformed gpt-oss
on science/knowledge and mathematics.
Verified fact — Artificial Analysis also attributes Chinese adoption partly to
openness. Its China report says DeepSeek and Alibaba have both embraced openweight strategies, supporting widespread domestic and international adoption; it also
states that China’s open-weight frontier surpassed the U.S. open-weight frontier in late
2024.

Interpretation — when the gap probably matters. The retrieved evidence says the
gap matters more for agentic coding, cyber/security-sensitive
tasks, search/retrieval-heavy assistants, and very difficult reasoning benchmarks
such as HLE.
Interpretation — when the gap may matter less. The gap is smaller for questionanswer style knowledge tasks, some general drafting/summarisation-like
workloads, and some UI/web coding arenas where well-tuned open/open-weight
models remain very competitive.
Interpretation — the strongest careful formulation. A rigorous claim supported by
the retrieved material would be: “Near-frontier and open-weight models often trail
frontier proprietary models on broad capability composites and agentic
benchmarks; however, for many knowledge-heavy and some coding workloads,
the observed gap is substantially smaller than headline ‘frontier gap’ narratives
suggest.”
